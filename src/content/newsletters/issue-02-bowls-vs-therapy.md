People often ask a fair question: *If a sound bath makes me feel calmer, isn’t that evidence?*

Felt change matters in a human life. It is not the same thing as **clinical evidence** that an intervention treats a disease. Sound Evidence exists to hold both truths without marketing fog.

### What the bowl literature actually looks like

Cai and colleagues (2025) reviewed nineteen clinical studies of singing bowls. Every assessed RCT was **high risk of bias** — especially around outcome measurement — and blinding is nearly impossible. Heterogeneity blocked most meta-analyses. The authors’ own conclusion is potential, not confirmation.

Goldsby et al. (2017) is widely cited in wellness circles: sixty-two people, one ~60-minute group session, large drops in tension and anxiety scales. There was **no control group**. Rest, expectancy, group setting, and time could explain much of the shift. Mechanism paragraphs about binaural beats or a “biofield” are speculation, not findings.

### What sits higher on the ladder

Credentialed **music therapy** plus usual care has a different evidence file — still imperfect, still mostly short-term, but anchored in Cochrane-level synthesis for some mood outcomes (see Aalbers 2017). NCCIH’s Music and Health overview is a good institutional map for anxiety, pain, sleep, and dementia questions.

### FTC-safe posture

We describe bowls as a **wellness / ritual** practice with **weak** clinical evidence. We do not claim they treat Parkinson’s, autism, hypertension, or insomnia. We do not paste vibroacoustic “40 Hz” talking points onto crystal bowls (Kantor 2022 studied a different modality and still called the pain evidence sparse).

Respect for the practice can look like better consent language: *many people find this calming; research is early and biased; this is not medical treatment.* That sentence is kinder — and safer — than a miracle claim.
