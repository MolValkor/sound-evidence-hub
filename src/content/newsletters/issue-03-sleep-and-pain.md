Two of the cleaner stories in music-and-health research are **subjective sleep** and **perceived chronic pain**. Both still want the word **adjunct**.

### Sleep (Jespersen 2022, Cochrane)

Thirteen trials, about a thousand adults, often listening to prerecorded music for 25–60 minutes. Moderate-certainty improvement on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. **No clear effect** on insomnia severity scales. Objective sleep measures in three small studies did not improve. No adverse events jumped out in the included papers — but you also cannot blind people to music, so expectancy is baked in.

Translation for real life: a wind-down playlist may help some people *feel* they slept better. That is not the same as curing insomnia disorder or replacing CBT-I or clinical care.

### Chronic pain (Chen 2025, BMC Psychology)

Nine RCTs, 787 patients. Pooled pain SMD around −0.51 — a moderate average effect with notable heterogeneity. Depression looked promising in a smaller subset of trials but with high inconsistency. Anxiety and quality-of-life analyses were underpowered and not statistically significant.

Honest use: music listening as a **complement** to a pain plan. Dishonest use: “ditch your meds and play 528 Hz.” We will never publish the second sentence.

### Dementia depression, briefly

The 2025 Cochrane update on music-based interventions in dementia (van der Steen et al.) found a **slight** moderate-certainty improvement in depressive symptoms in mostly care-home settings — and little lasting effect after treatment stops. Small and real is still small.
