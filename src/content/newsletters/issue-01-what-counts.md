Welcome to **Sound Evidence** — a small companion library for people who care about music, sound practices, and honest science communication.

We are not a clinic, not a sales funnel for singing bowls, and not a clone of any course brand. We collect peer-reviewed landmarks, institutional briefs, and labeled exploratory notes so you can see the difference between a **Cochrane systematic review** and a **pre–post sound meditation with no control group**.

### A simple ladder

1. **Strong / mixed toward strong** — multiple trials plus a careful systematic review with moderate-or-better certainty for a defined intervention and outcome. Example territory: short-term adjunct music therapy for depressive symptoms (Aalbers 2017).
2. **Mixed** — real signals, but low certainty, high heterogeneity, fading effects, or subjective yes / objective no. Example: music for subjective sleep quality (Jespersen 2022); music-based therapy for dementia depression (van der Steen 2025).
3. **Weak** — small samples, uncontrolled designs, or all-high risk-of-bias RCTs. Example: singing bowls (Cai 2025; Goldsby 2017); vibroacoustic therapy for pain (Kantor 2022).
4. **None for medical claims** — cure/treat/diagnose language for bowls, 432/528 Hz mythology, or “energy fields.” We do not go there.

### Language we use on purpose

- **Adjunct** and **short-term** when the review supports that framing.
- **Educational, not medical advice** on every relevant page.
- **Licensed music therapy ≠ sound bath** — AMTA and NCCIH both draw that line; we do too.

If you leave this issue with one habit, make it this: when a headline says music or bowls “heal” a disease, ask for the study design, the control group, and the certainty label. That habit is the whole site in miniature.

*— Sound Evidence*
